Desk CubeSat

STM32PCB DesignReaction WheelAttitude Control
IN PROGRESS — architecture & driver schematic done, PCB + firmware underway

Overview

A satellite-styled desk device that shows the time, date, and ambient conditions at rest — and doubles as a working 3-axis reaction-wheel attitude control testbed, complete with a custom motor driver PCB, real-time torque sensing, and closed-loop orientation feedback. It started as a desk gadget: a small satellite-shaped enclosure showing room temperature, time, and date on a display. It grew from there into a functional attitude-control test platform, with the body sitting on a rotating test ring driven by three reaction wheels so it can demonstrate real spacecraft-style attitude maneuvers — reorienting itself using only internal momentum, no external thrust — on a desk.

The problem

A single reaction wheel gets you rotation around one axis; doing it properly — the way an actual spacecraft does — means controlling all three axes independently and closing the loop with real orientation feedback instead of just commanding motor speed and hoping. That meant sourcing torque feedback per motor, fusing it with a 9-DOF orientation sensor, and running all of it through a microcontroller with enough ADC channels to read three motor drivers at once. The DRV8874 motor driver IC fit the torque-sensing requirement well via its IPROPI current-sense output, but proved difficult to find as a pre-built breakout board — which is what pushed the project toward a fully custom motor driver PCB instead of an off-the-shelf module.

System architecture

The schematic is organized hierarchically: a top-level sheet ties together a Power sub-sheet, three identical Motor Controller sub-sheets (one per reaction wheel), an STM pins sheet that breaks out the NUCLEO-G474RE's connections, and a Sensors sheet reserved for the BNO055, BME280, OLED, ESP32-C3, touch sensor, and status LED — that sheet is still an empty placeholder, since the sensor side hasn't been wired up yet. Right now the schematic fully covers power and all three motor channels; sensors are the next thing to design.

Each motor sub-sheet exposes the same set of signals back to the STM32: a current-sense line (CS), an enable line (EN), a fault line (FAULT), and a direction/phase line (PH), with a single SLEEP_n line shared across all three drivers. The core NUCLEO-G474RE is split across two symbol halves (U4A/U4B) on the STM pins sheet, since its Morpho connector has more pins than fit cleanly on one symbol — U4A carries the fault/enable lines for channels 1 and 3 plus power, and U4B carries the remaining current-sense, enable, and phase lines along with the SDA/SCL bus reserved for the (not-yet-wired) sensor bus.

Planned sensor integration (not yet wired)

The Sensors sheet is still an empty placeholder, but the plan is to hang orientation and environmental sensing off the I2C bus already stubbed out on the STM pins sheet, add an SPI display, and use a UART-linked co-processor for WiFi time sync:

Motor driver design

Each of the three reaction wheels gets its own custom driver board built around the DRV8874PWPR (U2). Since a pre-built breakout wasn't readily available, the circuit was built up from scratch: a P-channel MOSFET (Q2, AO3401A) sits in the VIN line ahead of the driver for reverse-polarity protection, a 16.2V zener (D2) clamps input transients, and a bulk capacitor bank (C7–C10: two 0.1µF and two 4.7µF) filters the supply. A 220nF cap (C11) and 22nF cap (C12) condition the VCP charge-pump and CPH/CPL pins the DRV8874 needs for its high-side gate drive. IPROPI current-sense feedback is conditioned through R11 (2.49kΩ) and R12 (20kΩ) before heading to the STM32's ADC as the real-time torque signal, IMODE is left unpopulated (R9, NP) to select the default current-sense gain, and PMODE is tied to ground as required by the datasheet. SLEEP_N gets a 10kΩ pull (R10) and FAULT_N a 10kΩ pull-up (R8) to 3.3V so a fault reads as a clean logic level back to the MCU. Motor output (OUT1/OUT2) lands on a 2-pin screw terminal (J2).

Motor selection

Direct-drive brushed DC motors — no gearbox — were chosen for control responsiveness, since gearbox backlash delays torque reaching the flywheel and would work against the closed-loop control the whole system depends on.

No-load speed14,974 RPM
Stall torque34.5 mN·m

That stall current sits within the DRV8874's 6A peak rating but is close enough to its 3.5A continuous limit that software-based stall protection is planned rather than relying on the driver's hardware limits alone.

Power architecture

Because the body rotates continuously and without limit during testing, the whole system runs on an onboard 3S LiPo battery rather than tethered wall power — a wired tether or slip ring would be impractical for a hobby build that's meant to spin freely and indefinitely. The 12V motor rail comes straight off the battery (J6), and a TPS54360DDA synchronous buck converter (U5) steps that down to a clean 5V logic rail. The feedback divider (R20/R21) sets the 5V regulation point, R19 and the RT/CLK pin set the switching frequency, and an LC output filter (L1, 8.2µH, plus the C24/C25 output capacitor bank) keeps ripple down. A resistor divider (R22/R23/R24) off the enable pin sets the UVLO threshold so the converter stays off until the input is within range, a bootstrap capacitor (C22, 0.1µF) drives the high-side switch, and a Schottky diode (D4, B560C) sits across the switch node for transient protection.
