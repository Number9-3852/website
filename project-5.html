Dual-Rail Power Supply Board

KiCadPower ElectronicsPCB Design

Overview

A small 2-layer PCB that takes a single 12V input and breaks it out into regulated 5V and 3.3V rails. I designed it to power a robotics project that needed three different voltage domains at once: 12V for a DC motor, 5V for servo motors, and 3.3V for sensors and the microcontroller — so instead of juggling three separate supplies on the bench, everything now runs off one 12V source through this board.

The problem

The rest of the project had power requirements that didn't share a common rail: a 12V DC motor for actuation, 5V-logic servos, and a 3.3V microcontroller and sensor suite. I needed something compact and efficient enough to sit on the robot itself, not a wall of bench supplies, and reliable enough that a noisy motor rail wouldn't corrupt the sensor readings on the 3.3V side.

What I built

The 12V input feeds a TPS54360 synchronous buck converter (U1) that steps it down to 5V. The output feedback divider (R4/R5) sets the regulation point, R6 and the RT/CLK pin set the switching frequency, and an LC output filter (L1, 8.2µH, plus the C6/C7 output capacitor bank) keeps ripple in check. A resistor divider off VIN (R1/R2/R3) sets the enable/UVLO threshold so the converter stays off until the input is within a safe range, and a bootstrap capacitor (C5) drives the high-side switch. A Schottky diode (D1) sits across the switch node for extra protection during transients.

The regulated 5V rail is broken out directly for the servos, and also feeds an AMS1117-3.3 linear regulator (U2) to derive the 3.3V rail for the microcontroller and sensors. Using the switching regulator's 5V output as the LDO's input — rather than dropping straight from 12V to 3.3V linearly — keeps the LDO's dropout losses small since it's only shedding 1.7V instead of 8.7V.

Layout-wise, the board is a compact 2-layer design with the three connectors (12V in, 5V out, 3.3V out) as screw terminals along the edges, keeping the switching node and inductor loop tight to minimize EMI.

Outcome

The board now sits between the 12V supply and the rest of the robotics project, giving the motor, servos, and control electronics each a clean, appropriately-regulated rail from a single input — no more running separate bench supplies for testing.
